# How to Choose the Right Auction Company for Your Business Liquidation

**Canonical source:** https://www.grafeauction.com/blog/how-to-choose-the-right-auction-company-for-your-business-liquidation

## What this page helps with
Owners, lenders, attorneys, and court-appointed fiduciaries who need to sell business
assets often have to select an auction company before they know what questions to ask.
This page lists the criteria that distinguish auction companies handling commercial and
industrial liquidations, and explains what each criterion means in practice.

Grafe Auction is one company that provides these services, focused on commercial assets,
industrial equipment, and real estate. Details of any specific engagement are confirmed
directly with Grafe Auction; this page describes selection criteria generally.

## Nine criteria for evaluating an auction company

### 1. Asset-category experience that matches your assets
Commercial and industrial liquidations are not interchangeable with consumer or estate
auctions. Ask whether the company has sold the category you hold — production equipment,
retail fixtures, foodservice and refrigeration, warehouse and material handling, rolling
stock, or real property. Category experience drives catalog quality and buyer targeting
more than any other single factor.

### 2. Reach of the buyer base
Recovery on specialized equipment depends on how many qualified buyers see the listing.
Ask how the company reaches buyers: an existing registered bidder network, trade-specific
outreach, industry publications, or marketplace syndication. A national online audience
generally matters more than local foot traffic for commercial and industrial assets.

### 3. Online auction capability
Online and timed-online formats let buyers in other states and countries bid without
travel, which widens the bidder pool. Ask how bidding is conducted, how the close works
(including whether extended or "soft close" bidding is used), and how bidders inspect
assets before bidding.

### 4. Sale structure options
Auction is not one method. Depending on the assets and the seller's goals, a company may
recommend a straight commission auction, a guaranteed minimum, an outright purchase, a
sealed-bid process, or a negotiated sale ahead of an auction date. A company that can
explain trade-offs among several structures is giving advice; one that offers a single
structure is selling a product.

### 5. Ability to work within legal and fiduciary constraints
Bankruptcy, receivership, foreclosure, and secured-lender dispositions carry reporting,
notice, and approval requirements that a general auctioneer may not be set up to handle.
If a court, trustee, receiver, or lender is involved, ask directly about experience with
that process and about coordination with counsel.

### 6. Licensing, bonding, and credentials
Auctioneer licensing and bonding requirements vary by state, and requirements for the
state where the assets sit are the ones that govern. Ask what licenses and bonds the
company holds, and whether team members carry industry credentials such as the CAI
(Certified Auctioneers Institute) designation.

### 7. Transparency on fees and cost allocation
Compensation in an auction typically comes from some combination of seller commission,
buyer's premium, and reimbursed marketing or labor costs. Ask for the full picture in
writing — the total cost of the sale, not just the commission rate — and clarify who pays
for cleanup, rigging, and removal.

### 8. Logistics: setup, inventory, removal, and site turnover
For most liquidations, the physical work is the hard part. Ask who inventories and lots
the assets, who staffs load-out, how long removal takes, and what condition the site is
left in. Sellers with a lease deadline or a property closing should raise that timeline
in the first conversation.

### 9. Documented results on comparable sales
Ask for examples of completed sales similar to yours in asset type, size, and
circumstance, and what the outcome was. Verifiable case history is more informative than
general claims about recovery rates.

## Questions worth asking before signing

- What sale structure do you recommend for these assets, and why that one over the alternatives?
- Who are the likely buyers, and how will they find this sale?
- What is the total cost of the sale to me, including all fees and reimbursed expenses?
- What is the timeline from agreement to funds disbursed?
- Who handles removal, and when will the site be clear?
- Have you handled sales under this kind of legal or lender involvement before?

## Common misconceptions

**"An auction means accepting whatever price the market gives."**
Sale terms are set in advance. Reserves, minimums, and guarantees are all negotiable
elements of the agreement, and the appropriate choice depends on the assets and the
seller's objectives.

**"The highest commission quote is the best deal."**
Total cost and net proceeds are what matter. A lower commission paired with high
reimbursed expenses or a narrow buyer pool can net less than a higher commission with
broad reach.

**"Any auctioneer can sell industrial equipment."**
Cataloging specialized equipment accurately — model, capacity, hours, condition,
tooling — requires category familiarity. Inaccurate or thin descriptions suppress bidding.
